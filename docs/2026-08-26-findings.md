# What held up — 26 August 2026

Every test below was registered and committed before it ran. Each registration commit is an ancestor
of its own results commit, so "we registered this first" is checkable rather than asserted.

11 underlyings, 30 months of inverted implied volatility, 24 commits.

---

## The premise holds

Rank names by how rich their implied volatility is against their own history, and the top of that
ranking retains more of the premium it sells.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Rank IC** | **+0.1753** — IV percentile vs. premium retained, 1,807 name-days |
| **Control arm** | **−0.1055** — same test on *raw* IV level |
| **Permutation p** | **0.0010** — beat all 1,000 within-day shuffles, seed 42 |

The control arm is what makes this believable. A positive IC on its own proves very little here,
because implied volatility sits on both sides of the obvious test. So we ran the same measurement
against the raw IV level, and it came back negative: selling whatever happens to have the highest
absolute volatility is mildly harmful. Whatever edge exists lives in the comparison to a name's own
history, not in volatility being high.

## And it isn't an earnings detector

The strongest objection anyone raised was that "this name's IV is unusually high" mostly means
"earnings are coming". If that were true, filtering earnings out wouldn't protect the strategy. It
would delete it.

| Sample | Name-days | Rank IC | Perm p |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| All name-days | 1,807 | **+0.1753** | 0.0010 |
| ±2 sessions around events removed | 1,674 | **+0.1831** | 0.0010 |
| Forward window entirely event-free | 1,145 | **+0.1561** | 0.0010 |

Our first attempt at this was too weak to mean much. Excluding two sessions either side of an
announcement reached only 7.4% of the contamination, because the outcome measures volatility over
the following 21 sessions and an announcement lands inside that window even when the entry sits
weeks away. The powered version throws out 36.6% of the sample. The result survives it. Earnings
dates came from SEC 8-K Item 2.02 filings, where the filing date is the announcement date.

## Execution costs what the book charges, not what the spec says

Two live round trips, with the NBBO captured at every submission because it can't be reconstructed
afterwards.

| Underlying | Leg spreads | Limit at mid | Execution vs mid |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| SPY | 3¢ | Filled better than mid, 127 ms | **+0.015** |
| AMD | 14–66¢ | Rested 26 s, never filled | **−0.310** |

Fills clear better than the touch by a small absolute amount, and that amount doesn't scale with how
wide the book is. On a tight book execution ends up earning; on a wide one it costs. Anything fitted
to SPY alone would be badly wrong for the rest of the universe. Fees came out exact: $0.025 per
contract-leg, identical residual across two trades differing ninefold in notional.

## Where we were wrong

Two of these would have shipped a headline number that meant nothing, and both were caught by
self-audit after the run rather than by anyone reviewing the work.

The first was an arithmetic error dressed up as a finding. We reported that execution beats a
bid/ask backtest by 0.165, about 27% of premium, having subtracted a cost from a P&L that already
contained a market move. Like-for-like it was 0.100. The whole comparison was then superseded when a
second round trip showed the effect was specific to SPY.

The second was worse, because the number looked plausible. We measured the signal's information
coefficient at 0.16 using an outcome and a signal that were both functions of the same
implied-volatility term. The statistic was measuring itself. It got voided and re-registered with a
scale-free outcome and the control arm described above.

Three smaller ones:

- A measurement-noise ratio we'd registered as a diagnostic got quietly promoted to an eligibility
  filter. Applying it left a one-name universe, which is what prompted us to test it properly: the
  choice of price print moves the ranking by 2–5 percentile points against a noise level of 29.
- We declared historical spreads unestimable on the strength of a proxy correlating +0.036 across
  names, having only tried bar statistics. Roll's estimator on trade sequences reaches +0.500. That
  correction needs a correction of its own: adversarial review found we'd reported it from an
  uncommitted script, so nobody could recompute it. It reproduces now, but at n=11 the permutation
  p is 0.0604, and the estimator goes silent more often on wide books than tight ones. A lead,
  not a result.
- We read a five-day lag into the option bar data. There was no lag. `limit` truncates from the
  start of the window, so the single bar we got back was the oldest rather than the newest.

---

## What is not established

- No backtested return is quotable. The spread estimator isn't fitted, and one calibrated on a
  single session isn't a calibration.
- The structure and strike selection have never been reviewed. An adversarial review was running
  when this was written.
- The scheduled cycle has never placed an order. Both round trips were placed by hand.
- The universe was chosen on today's liquidity and applied to 2024–25 data. Survivorship, disclosed.
- A 30-day reference tenor stands in for a 5–9 day traded structure, deliberately, and we haven't
  tested whether that substitution holds.

---

## Method

Three of the registrations written today still failed, and none of them failed on their thresholds.
One decision table omitted the outcome that actually occurred, then a second one did the same thing.
A third registration specified a statistic that shared a term with its own outcome. We were careful
about the numbers and careless about the scaffolding around them, which is worth knowing for next
time.

Figures are recomputable from the committed series and the raw order records. Registrations and
results live in `docs/probes/`, the cost model in `docs/cost-model.md`.
